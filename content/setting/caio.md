**Summary**: The Caio, Althas's road folk, and the wandering trickster they follow, whom the drabardi call Drabalo among a dozen other names. ---

The Caio are no distant people. Their caravans cross every settled land, their drabardi read fortunes in [[armada|Armada's]] markets, and Althas has known them for centuries. Yet of what the Caio actually believe, Althan scholarship holds almost nothing it trusts. The road-people keep their faith in the telling, not in any book, and keep it close from outsiders who have mostly written it down to mock it: a paragraph of caravan superstition in a merchant's ledger, a heresiarch's name in a See docket, a traveler's note on the fortune-tellers' tricks. What follows is reconstructed from that thin and condescending record, and from the drabardi who, now and then, have chosen to say a little. No scholar would call it complete, and the Caio would likely laugh at the parts that are wrong.

## Drabalo, and his many names

Every Caio child knows the shape of the story before they know it is a god's. A stranger comes to a hard road or a village the powerful have wrung dry, and leaves it a little better than he found it: a debt undone by a trick, a tyrant made a fool, a night of stories that sends people to bed laughing. Then he is gone before dawn. The Caio call that stranger **Drabalo**, the name the drabardi keep for him, and hold that he has walked Althas since the Divine Age and walks it still. He answers to many names besides, and his true one is held by no one, for a man who owns no name owns no home and is welcome at every fire. The road-people take him for a god; Althan scholarship, which knows the type, takes him for a good story, and grants at most that whatever sits beneath the story, if anything does, is past a scholar's reach.

## The road as the faith

What the Caio take from Drabalo is less a creed than a way to live, and they live it whether or not they would call it worship. The world, in their telling, is meant to be open, a place of wonder and welcome that the settled and the powerful are forever trying to wall off and tax. To be Caio is to refuse that. A Caio travels free and bows to no ruler as a better, helps whoever they find in want, the way Drabalo does, and tries to leave the world kinder than they found it. Luck and a quick tongue are what the road-people have against those who would stop them, and both, they say, are Drabalo's gifts: the trickster favors the traveler who slips the toll and the beggar who out-talks the magistrate.

## The Dromardo, the Open Road

The Caio call their way the **Dromardo**, the Open Road: not a scripture to recite but the sum of the customs a road-life runs on, lived until it is hard to say where habit ends and worship begins. A place is kept at the fire and a share of the food set by for the traveler who has not yet arrived, for any stranger on the road might be Drabalo walking hidden, and to turn one away is the nearest thing the Caio hold to a true sin. A wager is half a prayer: to bet, and to take the outcome in good humor, is to put a question to Drabalo, whom they hold the god of luck, and abide his answer. Charms and knots ride on the wagons for a safe road, and the Caio leave small marks at crossroads and camp-grounds, a scratched sign, a stack of stones, a knotted stem of grass, that tell the next caravan what the last one learned. And above all there is the telling: a night of stories and song is the Caio's truest devotion, for Drabalo is the first teller, and to keep his tales alive, and to add your own, is to keep him walking.

## The road as refuge

The [[the-holy-see|See]] takes a people who welcome anyone for a people with no standards; to the Caio, that welcome is the point. Their families are held loosely, blood carries no special claim, and a stranger willing to learn the ways of the road is taken in as kin, which has made the Caio a refuge for those Althas has cast out or worn down. The clearest case is the **Firbolg** of the south. A [[witherwild-continent|Witherwild]] people by origin, they were carried off that continent by the colonizers of [[haven|Haven]] and set to work the vineyards of the [[serracorte|Serracorte]] in bondage, an enslaved labor-class raising the wine that made [[andaluria|Andaluria]] rich. When slavery was at last outlawed the freed Firbolg found the settled country had little place for them, and the Caio caravans that had always passed through the vineyard highlands did. Generations on, the Firbolg of [[hilltop|Hilltop]] are Caio in nearly everything, keeping the Dromardo and the drabardi's craft beside the older [[the-faunus|Wickling ways]] they never wholly set down. To the Caio they are proof of the thing they most believe about themselves: that the open road can give the discarded a home when neither throne nor altar will.

## The drabardi

Where the faith takes visible shape, it is around the **drabardi**, the fortune-tellers who read the threads of fate in cards, bones, tea leaves, and the fall of dice. They are the nearest thing the Caio have to clergy, and they hold that in a well-read fortune one glimpses Drabalo's own hand, the luck he deals along the road. A drabardi's knowledge, the old readings and the older deals, passes down within families and is hoarded as the most valuable thing a caravan owns, and its keepers are among the wealthiest and most respected Caio there are. Most Armadans, Caio or not, take a drabardi's word seriously; the [[the-holy-see|See]] does not, and names the craft what it names all unlicensed magic. The drabardi answer, when they answer at all, that they read the world's luck rather than command it, and that a god who deals the cards can hardly object to their being turned over.

## The See and the settled powers

To the [[the-holy-see|Holy See]] the whole of it is superstition at best and heresy at worst: a people who bow to no [[the-one-above|One Above]], keep witches for priests, and teach that defying lawful authority is a virtue. In the zealous heartlands a drabardi's cards can be reason enough for an inquisitor's attention, and the witch-craft the Caio shelter is hunted wherever the See's arm reaches. Yet the faith rarely sets itself up as a rival to the Church, and that is much of how it survives. A Caio will stand in a village chapel on a holy day and consult a drabardi the next morning and feel no contradiction in it; the road-people take what works from whatever is at hand and leave the theology to those with the leisure for it. Where the See is strong the faith goes quiet and portable, carried in habits and stories rather than temples, as a faith of the road is built to be.

## Armada, and the roots

There is an irony in the faith's own heartland that the Caio feel keenly. It was the Caio who founded [[armada|Armada]], seceding in the [[the-ophanim|Ophanim]] crisis to make the trade-cities of the south their own, and it is in Armada that they have done the one thing their whole tradition warns against: they have put down roots. The [[guild|Guild]] that governs the federation is Caio-built, and for all that its seats pass by token rather than blood, it is still a government, the settled power taxing the road it once travelled. Not every Caio is easy with this. The sharpest of them say plainly that Drabalo, if he walks at all, would know Armada's merchant-lords for exactly the sort he has spent an age making fools of, and that a people who set out to keep the world open have become the bureaucrats who fence it. Whether Armada is the Caio's great work or their quiet apostasy is an argument the road-people have with themselves, and have not settled.
