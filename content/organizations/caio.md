**Summary**: The Caio are Althas's road folk. They follow a wandering trickster the drabardi call Drabalo, one of a dozen names he goes by, and hold him to be a god who has walked the world since the Divine Age. The Caio keep their beliefs close, and outsiders have written down little of them, mostly as caravan superstition. Althan scholars grant only that if Drabalo is more than a story, nobody can say what he is.

---

Caio caravans cross every settled land, and their drabardi read fortunes in [[armada|Armada's]] markets. Althas has lived alongside them for centuries and still knows almost nothing reliable about what they believe. The Caio pass their faith on by word of mouth, never in writing, and say little of it to outsiders, who have mostly written it down to mock it. The record amounts to a paragraph on caravan superstition in a merchant's ledger, a heresiarch's name in a See docket, and a traveler's note on the fortune-tellers' tricks. What follows is pieced together from those scraps and from what a few drabardi have chosen to tell. It is far from complete, and the Caio would probably laugh at the parts it gets wrong.

## Drabalo, and his many names

Caio children learn the story long before they learn it is about a god. A stranger turns up on a hard road, or in a village the powerful have squeezed dry, and leaves the place a little better off. He undoes a debt with a trick, makes a fool of a tyrant, or tells stories until everyone goes to bed laughing, and he is gone by dawn. The drabardi call him **Drabalo**, and the Caio hold that he has walked Althas since the Divine Age and walks it still. He goes by many other names. Nobody knows his real one, and the Caio say that is why he is welcome at every fire: a man with no name has no home. They take him for a god. Althan scholars, who have met the type before, take him for a good story, and allow at most that if anything lies beneath it, a scholar has no way to reach it.

## The road as the faith

The Caio take from Drabalo a way of life more than a creed, and live it whether or not they would call it worship. In their telling the world is meant to be open, full of wonder and welcome, and the settled and the powerful keep trying to wall it off and tax it. Being Caio means refusing that. A Caio travels free, bows to no ruler as a better, helps anyone in need the way Drabalo does, and tries to leave the world kinder than they found it. Against those who would stop them, the road-people have luck and a quick tongue, and they count both as Drabalo's gifts. The trickster favors the traveler who slips the toll and the beggar who talks his way past the magistrate.

## The Dromardo, the Open Road

The Caio call their way the **Dromardo**, the Open Road. It is the set of customs road life runs on rather than a scripture, and the Caio have lived by it so long that habit and worship are hard to tell apart. They keep a place at the fire and set food aside for the traveler who has not yet arrived, since any stranger on the road might be Drabalo walking hidden. Turning a stranger away is the nearest thing the Caio have to a real sin. A wager is half a prayer: every bet puts a question to Drabalo, whom they hold to be the god of luck, and taking the result in good humor means accepting his answer. Charms and knots hang on the wagons for a safe road. At crossroads and campsites the Caio leave small marks for the next caravan, a scratched sign, a stack of stones or a knotted stem of grass, to pass on what the last one learned. Above all there is storytelling. A night of stories and song is the Caio's truest devotion, because Drabalo was the first storyteller, and keeping his tales alive and adding your own keeps him walking.

## The road as refuge

The [[the-holy-see|See]] sees a people who welcome anyone as a people with no standards. To the Caio, the welcome is the whole point. Family ties are loose among them, blood gives no special claim, and any stranger willing to learn the ways of the road is taken in as kin, so the Caio have become a refuge for people Althas has cast out or worn down. The [[firbolg|Firbolg]] of [[hilltop|Hilltop]] are the clearest example. They are a [[witherwild-continent|Witherwild]] people, carried off that continent by the colonizers of [[haven|Haven]] and forced to work the vineyards of the [[serracorte|Serracorte]] as slaves, raising the wine that made [[andaluria|Andaluria]] rich. When slavery was finally outlawed, the freed Firbolg found little room for them in the settled country, but the Caio caravans that had always passed through the vineyard highlands took them in. Generations later, the Firbolg of [[hilltop|Hilltop]] are Caio in nearly everything: they keep the Dromardo and the drabardi's craft alongside the older [[the-faunus|Wickling ways]] they never fully gave up. The Caio point to them as proof that the open road can give the discarded a home when neither throne nor altar will.

## The drabardi

The faith is most visible in the **drabardi**, the fortune-tellers who read fate in cards, bones, tea leaves and the fall of dice. They are the closest thing the Caio have to clergy, and they hold that a well-read fortune shows Drabalo's own hand, the luck he deals along the road. A drabardi's knowledge of the old readings and the older deals passes down within families, and a caravan guards it as its most valuable possession. Those who keep it are among the richest and most respected Caio. Most Armadans, Caio or not, take a drabardi's word seriously. The [[the-holy-see|See]] does not, and calls the craft what it calls all unlicensed magic. When the drabardi answer at all, they say they read the world's luck and do not command it, and that a god who deals the cards can hardly mind them being turned over.

## The See and the settled powers

To the [[the-holy-see|Holy See]] it is all superstition at best and heresy at worst: a people who bow to no [[the-one-above|One Above]], keep witches for priests, and teach that defying lawful authority is a virtue. In the zealous heartlands a drabardi's cards can be enough to draw an inquisitor's attention, and the See hunts the witch-craft the Caio shelter wherever its reach extends. Still, the faith rarely sets itself up against the Church, and that is a large part of why it survives. A Caio will stand in a village chapel on a holy day, consult a drabardi the next morning, and see no contradiction. The road-people take what works from whatever is at hand and leave theology to people with time for it. Where the See is strong, the faith goes quiet and portable, carried in habits and stories instead of temples.

## Armada, and the roots

The Caio feel an irony in their own heartland. They were among the founders of [[armada|Armada]], breaking away during the [[the-ophanim|Ophanim]] crisis beside merchant lords, pirates and southern nobles tired of the See's taxes, and in Armada they have done the one thing their tradition warns against: they have put down roots. The Caio helped build the [[guild|Guild]] that governs the federation, and although its seats pass by token and not by blood, it is still a government, a settled power taxing the road it once travelled. Not every Caio is comfortable with that. The sharpest critics say outright that Drabalo, if he walks at all, would see Armada's merchant-lords as exactly the kind of people he has spent an age making fools of, and that a people who set out to keep the world open have become the bureaucrats fencing it in. The Caio are still arguing among themselves over whether Armada is their great achievement or their quiet apostasy.
